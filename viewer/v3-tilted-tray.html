Sunken Basin, Tilted Tray — design study

One tier, six wells plus a centre well for a climber.     One basin, six floors. Where two wells overlap, the wall between them is gone and the water can pass — that is the continuous, carved-out look. But each well still has its own level floor, sitting one step below its uphill neighbour, and the neck between them acts as the weir. So the basin reads as one piece while every well independently holds its reserve: stop the pump and each one keeps its depth instead of draining down the slope into the glass.

    The recess is what makes an air pump viable. Sinking the wells does more than look better. An uplift tube can only discharge about 104 mm above the glass floor, because it has to stay roughly 70 % submerged in a reservoir only ~74 mm deep. Wells standing on the tray put their water far above that ceiling; wells sunk into it bring the water back down within reach. Deepen the recess and watch the verdict change.

    The tube is also the trellis. Running the uplift tube up the centre kills two problems at once: the feed never has to cross the glass rim to get to the wells, and the post it forms is something a climbing plant in the centre well can grow up. The spout height above the top well is adjustable — that is the "spout clearance".

    It prints flat, in one piece, without support. The tray lies in its own plane on the bed and every well is a pocket opening upward. The well floors are level in use, so relative to the tray they are slightly tilted — but they are cavity floors with solid material beneath them, not overhangs. That is the whole reason for sinking the wells rather than standing pots on top.

    The glass. Inner mouth Ø90 across, rim leaning 21.3° with a 35 mm rise, 80 mm from the base to the low side of the rim and 115 mm to the high side — all measured. The rim tilt is a slider so a different glass can be tried. The bulge of the body is interpolated from a photograph.
